Personal · Tennis

The situation can be difficult without the outcome being decided.

What tennis taught me · 3 min read
In this piece
  • Found in lockdown
  • The next point
  • Nobody can play it for you
  • Why Novak

I found tennis during the pandemic, which is to say I found it at exactly the moment I needed something that was about staying in a contest.

I don't love it purely as a spectacle. What holds me is what the sport asks of a person psychologically, which is more than almost anything else I watch.

Start with the structure. Losing a point does not decide anything. Neither does losing a set. A match can run for hours, momentum can swing four or five times, and there is no rule that effort converts into a result on any particular schedule. All you can really do is reset and play the next point, and keep doing that long enough to give yourself a chance. I find that idea genuinely consoling — that being behind is a description of the current situation, not a verdict.

Being behind is a description, not a verdict.

Then there is the accountability, which is the part I think about most. A player has a coach, a hitting partner, a physio, a team, family in the box. All of that matters. But once the match starts, nobody can play the point for them. They have to execute it themselves.

That distinction sits close to how I think about work. I value teams and I have learned a great deal from the people I have worked alongside. But being on a good team is not a substitute for being competent, prepared, and willing to own the outcome. The support system gets you to the baseline. It does not hit the ball.

And then competitiveness, which I would frame a little differently from working hard. Effort is the easy part. The harder thing is staying mentally in something for a long time while another person, or another set of circumstances, is actively working to make it not go your way. Tolerating the setback, adapting mid-match, and still wanting the outcome three hours in.

Which is why I am a Novak Djokovic fan, and it has never really been about the trophy count. What I admire is the mental toughness — the composure under pressure, the recovery when a match looks gone, the sheer difficulty of putting him away. The quality I am drawn to isn't winning. It's being extraordinarily hard to defeat.

What I take from it is not complicated. Resilience, sustained effort, and taking responsibility for the result — and the satisfaction of outlasting something difficult rather than being spared it.
